People sign in to the console through your identity provider, over OpenID
Connect. Panorama keeps no passwords and creates no accounts of its own: an
account appears the first time somebody signs in, and their role comes from
the groups your provider says they are in.

Single sign-on is the part of a deployment that most often goes wrong, and
usually silently. This section has three pages:

- **This page**: how it works, and the steps.
- **[Identity provider guides](/docs/configure/sso-providers/)**: what to do at
  Entra ID, Okta, Keycloak, Cognito, Auth0, PingOne and Google Workspace, and
  why each one behaves as it does.
- **[SSO troubleshooting](/docs/configure/sso-troubleshooting/)**: every
  message and every check, by symptom.

:::danger[AWS IAM Identity Center will not work]
Identity Center offers third-party apps SAML sign-in only, and SAML is not
implemented yet. It also has no groups attribute, so roles could not be mapped
through it even with a bridge. Point Panorama at the provider *behind* it:
Entra ID, Okta, your directory.
:::

## How sign-in works

Knowing these six things explains most of what goes wrong.

1. **Panorama is a confidential OIDC client** using the authorization code
   flow. It needs an issuer URL, a client id and a client secret.
2. **It reads the ID token, and only the ID token.** Not the access token and
   not the userinfo endpoint. A claim your provider puts anywhere else does not
   exist as far as Panorama is concerned.
3. **From that token it uses four things**: the subject (`sub`), the `email`,
   a display name (`name`, then `preferred_username`, then `given_name`), and
   one claim listing group membership, called `groups` unless you say
   otherwise.
4. **The role is recomputed from groups at every sign-in.** That is what makes
   removing somebody from a group at your provider remove their access here.
   If somebody is in several mapped groups, the strongest role wins.
5. **Group names are matched as plain strings, ignoring case.** Panorama does
   not know what a group *is* at your provider. It compares the strings in the
   token with the strings in your mapping. If your provider sends IDs, you map
   IDs.
6. **The first person ever to sign in becomes an administrator**, whatever
   their groups. Otherwise a new deployment would have nobody able to finish
   setting it up.

## Connect your provider

The connection lives in the deployment's configuration, beside every other
secret. The console checks it for you and never stores it.

| Setting | Environment variable | Default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Issuer URL | `PANORAMA_OIDC_ISSUER` | not set, which means SSO is off |
| Client id | `PANORAMA_OIDC_CLIENT_ID` | not set |
| Client secret | `PANORAMA_OIDC_CLIENT_SECRET`, or `PANORAMA_OIDC_CLIENT_SECRET_FILE` for a mounted file | not set |
| Scopes | `PANORAMA_OIDC_SCOPES` | `openid profile email groups` |
| Groups claim | `PANORAMA_OIDC_GROUPS_CLAIM` | `groups` |

The steps:

1. **Open the console's single sign-on screen.** On a new deployment, enter
   the one-time setup code. Later, it is **Settings → Single sign-on**.
2. **Choose your identity provider.** The screen shows what that provider
   needs, in order. [Identity provider guides](/docs/configure/sso-providers/)
   explains the reasons.
3. **Register an application at your provider**, with the **redirect URI**
   exactly as the screen shows it. It is your console's address followed by
   `/auth/oidc/callback`. A trailing slash makes it a different URI.
4. **Turn on group membership in the token.** Almost no provider sends groups
   by default. This is the step people miss, and missing it produces no error.
5. **Enter the issuer URL and client id, and press Check this connection.** It
   proves the issuer answers, and prints the configuration to apply. It saves
   nothing.
6. **Apply that configuration and restart.** Where it goes depends on how you
   deployed: [`.env` on a server](/docs/deploy/server/#4-connect-single-sign-on),
   or [a Secrets Manager secret on AWS](/docs/deploy/aws/#connect-single-sign-on).
7. **Sign in.**

From a shell, step 5 is `panorama sso probe`, which can print the configuration
in the shape your deployment wants (`-format env`, `ecs`, `shell` or `yaml`):

```sh
panorama sso probe -preset okta -issuer https://your-org.okta.com \
  -client-id panorama -format env
```

## Map groups to roles

There are three roles. **Admin** configures everything. **Manager** sees the
totals for teams they lead and sets limits inside them. **Developer** sees
their own usage and nothing else.

Group names can only be learned from a real sign-in, so sign in once first.
Then, in **Settings → Single sign-on**:

1. Look at **Groups your provider has sent**. These are the exact strings that
   arrived in tokens, which is the only reliable source: what your provider's
   own console displays is often not what it sends.
2. Click a group. The **Add a mapping** panel opens with it filled in.
3. Choose the **Role** and press **Add mapping**.
4. Set **Someone in no mapped group** to what should happen to everybody else.
   *Becomes a developer* is the usual choice. *Cannot sign in* turns the
   mapping into a strict allowlist.
5. Press **Save mapping**.

Roles change at each person's **next sign-in**, not immediately.

You can type a group nobody has signed in from yet. Be careful: a mistyped
group grants nothing and reports no error.

From a shell, `panorama sso claims` lists the same groups.

## Keep an administrator who does not depend on a group

Because roles are recomputed at every sign-in, an administrator whose group
membership changes stops being one. To pin somebody as an administrator
regardless of groups, use **Settings → Single sign-on → Direct administrators →
Choose**, tick the accounts, and **Save**.

**Hand back to SSO** returns an account to the mapping at their next sign-in.
Panorama refuses to hand back the last administrator when no group could grant
the role again.

Keep this list short. A pinned role is one your identity provider can no longer
revoke. Its main use is the account that owns a new deployment, before any
group maps to admin. Administrator is the only role that can be pinned.

## Limit sign-in to your domains

Most providers need nothing here. An Entra ID tenant, an Okta org, a Keycloak
realm or a Cognito pool already decides who can sign in, and its issuer URL
names it.

Set `PANORAMA_OIDC_ALLOWED_DOMAINS` when that is not enough:

```sh
PANORAMA_OIDC_ALLOWED_DOMAINS=example.com,example.org
```

- **Google Workspace: required.** Google has one issuer for every Google
  account there is, and the first account to sign in owns the deployment.
  Panorama refuses to start on that issuer without this. See
  [Google Workspace](/docs/configure/sso-providers/#google-workspace).
- **A tenant with guests or partner accounts** you do not want in Panorama.

Write each domain bare, with no `@`, no `https://` and no wildcard. The match
is exact, so `eu.example.com` is a different domain from `example.com`. On the
CloudFormation stack it is the `OidcAllowedDomains` parameter. Like the rest of
the connection it is read at startup, so restart after changing it.

Somebody outside the list sees *"Your account is not in a domain that may use
this Panorama deployment"*, and the log says which domain was refused. No
account is created.

## Check it worked

In **Settings → Single sign-on**, press **Verify**. It checks the redirect URI,
the secret, whether anybody has signed in, whether sign-ins carried groups, and
whether every mapped group has ever been seen. Each finding says what to do.

From a shell it is the same check, and it exits non-zero on failure, so it
works in a deployment pipeline:

```sh
panorama sso doctor
```

[SSO troubleshooting](/docs/configure/sso-troubleshooting/#what-verify-and-sso-doctor-report)
explains every finding.

## Good to know

- **The client secret is never written to the database**, which is why no
  screen asks for it. A secret in a database is a secret in every backup.
- **Panorama records the names of the claims and groups it has seen**, so it
  can offer them as a list. It never records a claim's value, and keeps no
  record of who is in which group.
- **A group may only be mapped once.** Matching ignores case, so `Platform` and
  `platform` are one group.
- **Changing somebody's role signs them out** of their other sessions.
- **Locked out?** `panorama admin setup` issues a one-time code at any time. It
  opens a session where you can fix the connection and make an account an
  administrator again.
